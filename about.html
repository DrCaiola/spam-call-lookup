Where the data comes from

Two public sources — and one thing we deliberately don't use.

🏛️

FCC complaint records

When someone in the US gets a robocall or spam call, they can report it to the Federal Communications Commission — the government agency that regulates phones. The FCC publishes every complaint (minus personal details) in a free public database: the number that called, what kind of call it was, when, and what state it was reported from.

This site searches that database live — the moment you look up a number, your browser asks the FCC's servers directly. We never make a copy, so results are always current.

📖

The phone system's own directory

Every North American phone number starts with an area code and a three-digit prefix. Those aren't random: an official registry (NANPA, the organization that manages phone numbers for the US and Canada) records which phone company owns each prefix and which town it belongs to.

We keep a copy of that registry here, and a robot updates it from the source every Monday. It's how the site can say “this prefix belongs to T-Mobile in Colorado Springs” — or warn you that a prefix doesn't exist at all, which means the caller ID was faked.

🚫

What we don't use: paid name lookups

Those “find out who owns this number!” sites charge money because the actual name attached to a number is private data owned by phone carriers, who sell access to it. It is not public record.

We think the question you actually want answered — “is this call spam?” — doesn't need any of that. The public data answers it for free.

🔍

Check our work

Every line of code behind this site is published openly on GitHub, including the scripts that fetch the data. Anyone can read it, verify it, or run their own copy.

No accounts, no ads, no analytics, no cookies. Hosting a page like this costs nothing, so nothing needs to be paid for with your data.

What it can — and can't — tell you

Honesty matters more than looking clever. Here are the limits, plainly.

  • ✅Lots of complaints = strong signal. If hundreds of people across dozens of states reported a number, you can be confident calls from it are unwanted.
  • ⚠️Zero complaints doesn't mean safe. Spammers switch numbers constantly, and brand-new spam numbers haven't been reported yet. Treat any unknown caller with normal caution.
  • ⚠️Caller ID can be faked. Scammers often display someone else's real number (even Apple's official support line gets impersonated). A heavily-reported number might belong to an innocent party whose number was spoofed.
  • ⚠️The carrier shown can be out of date. When people switch phone companies they keep their number, so the company listed for a prefix is who it was originally assigned to.
  • ✅Your reports make it better. The database is built from ordinary people taking a minute to file a complaint. When you report a spam call, the next person who looks it up gets a better answer.

Your privacy

  • 🔒The number you type is sent to exactly one place: the FCC's public database, to search it. It isn't logged, stored, or sent anywhere else — this site has no server of its own to store anything on.
  • 🔒No sign-up, no cookies, no trackers. We can't see who you are or what you searched — and neither can anyone else here, because “here” is just a folder of files.
